Behar · Leviticus 25:1–26:2

Parasha Behar

The Sabbatical year, the Jubilee, and the laws of property, debt, and servitude.

Overview

Parasha Behar introduces two of the Torah's most revolutionary social institutions: the Shemitah (Sabbatical year) — when the land rests every seventh year — and the Yovel (Jubilee) — when every fiftieth year all land returns to its original owners and all Hebrew servants go free.

The Story

Every seventh year, the land of Israel must lie fallow — no plowing, planting, or harvesting. God promises to provide enough in the sixth year for three years. After seven cycles of seven years, the fiftieth year is the Jubilee: all land reverts to its ancestral owners, all debts are released, and all Hebrew servants are freed. The parasha also contains laws against charging interest to a fellow Israelite and the obligation to redeem a poor relative's land. The theological basis: "The land is Mine; you are strangers and residents with Me" (Leviticus 25:23).

Significance for Noahides

The Sabbatical and Jubilee years express the Torah's radical vision of economic justice: no permanent poverty, no permanent slavery, no permanent concentration of wealth. The declaration that the land belongs to God — and humans are merely stewards — is a universal principle with profound implications for how we relate to property, the environment, and the poor.
